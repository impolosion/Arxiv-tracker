LIBERO-PeRM: Benchmarking Personalized Robotic Manipulation
Authors: Zhixu Li, Keqian Tang, Litian Gong, Jingyu Yao, Wenqian Zhang, Tianze Xu, Zehao Wang, Yuping Wang, Junge Zhang, Jiachen Li
Venue: NeurIPS 2026
First: 2026-10-15T17:59:59+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:59:59+00:00
Comments: Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2026); Project Website: https://libero-perm.github.io/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Personalization is essential for general-purpose robots to transition into household environments and achieve true practicality. While general robotic manipulation has been extensively studied, personalized robotic manipulation (PeRM) lacks a controlled benchmark for separating task completion from preference satisfaction. We introduce LIBERO-PeRM, a benchmark for preference learning in embodied agents. LIBERO-PeRM studies five questions: 1) how policies follow preferences in zero-shot settings; 2) how they learn single preferences from demonstrations; 3) how efficiently they acquire preferences as demonstrations increase; 4) how learned preferences generalize across layouts and tasks; and 5) how policies compose preferences and behave under conflicts. We propose a controlled formulation and taxonomy of observable preferences in simulation, covering motion, skill, and user-profile choices. This formulation is restricted to language-expressible preferences that can be instantiated in LIBERO without changing the task goal, evaluated automatically, and reused across tasks. Building on LIBERO, our procedural pipeline generates paired demonstrations with and without preferences. We create five task suites (312 tasks) with high-quality demonstrations, rich metadata, and preference-satisfaction metrics. Experiments show that current VLA policies struggle with zero-shot personalization, while SFT makes preferences learnable. Mixed training achieves better no-preference retention than sequential adaptation and improves performance with contextual prompts compared with preference-only training, but can weaken motion-preference learning; preference generalization, decomposition, and conflict handling remain difficult. The code, datasets, and demos can be found on the project website: https://libero-perm.github.io/.

GRASP: Plan-Guided Graph Retrieval with Adaptive Fusion and Reranking on Semi-Structured Knowledge Bases
Authors: Yicheng Tao, Yiqun Wang, Xiangchen Song, Xin Luo, Kai Liu, Yuanhao Huang, Jie Liu
First: 2026-05-28T17:07:32+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:59:16+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Retrieval over semi-structured knowledge bases (SKBs) must handle both textual descriptions and relational constraints. Text-based retrievers capture the overall semantics of a query but struggle with multi-hop relations, whereas graph-based retrievers follow relational paths precisely but miss the broader meaning. We propose GRASP, a hybrid retriever that learns to fuse the two. A small LLM planner translates the query into an executable graph plan in a single call, its graph ranking is fused with a text ranking, and a fine-tuned reranker reorders the fused candidates. Instead of training the planner to be accurate on its own, we optimize it with reinforcement learning to maximize the gain its graph ranking brings to the fused ranking. On three STaRK benchmarks, GRASP achieves the best results on all metrics, improving the average Hit@1 by 18.6 points over the strongest baseline, while taking only 2.47 seconds per query.

Robust Reinforcement Learning for Dynamic Treatment Regimes under Model and Measurement Uncertainty

Abstract
We develop a robust reinforcement learning (RL) framework for learning adaptive treatment policies that generalize across patients and remain reliable under errors in the measured biomarker and shifts in the disease dynamics. By modeling the patient as a hidden context, we address generalization and robustness in a unified manner through robust contextual Markov decision processes. Applying our framework to adaptive therapy for metastatic prostate cancer in a simulator calibrated to clinical data, we obtain generalizable and robust policies that substantially outperform the existing adaptive therapy method. In particular, we attain robustness with almost no loss of performance when the biomarker is accurate and the disease evolves as anticipated, and find that the resulting policy Pareto-dominates the existing method across a diverse array of scenarios. Our findings suggest that robust RL holds great promise as a principled methodology for learning reliable adaptive treatment policies.

Co-Evolving Acting and Forecasting for Language Agents
Authors: Haofei Yu, Yixing Chen, Yining Zhao, Xuyan Ye, Yuxiang Liu, Zhigang Hua, Yan Xie, Shuang Yang, Jiaxuan You
First: 2026-10-14T17:59:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:52:52+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Training language agents is bottlenecked by the high cost of long-horizon rollouts, yet standard agentic reinforcement learning (RL) learns only from a terminal scalar reward, leaving the supervision in intermediate steps largely unused. Prior work exploits this supervision through value targets that estimate per-step success probabilities or observation targets that fit environment response. Both are hard to construct well: (1) value targets come from the same single scalar signal for each step in the trajectory, and (2) observation targets, such as web pages and terminal logs, are long and noisy and reach only one step ahead. We propose CoPE, which adds a third kind of multi-step action targets. On successful on-policy trajectories, CoPE trains the policy backbone to generate the action sequences that follows each history. Each action is also supervised when forecast from the preceding histories, forming a temporal ensemble that the shared backbone absorbs during training, and this enables acting and forecasting to co-evolve. The policy still acts one step at a time at inference, with no overhead. Across five benchmarks (ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, WebShop, $τ^2$-bench, and AppWorld), CoPE (1) achieves stronger aggregate performance than GRPO and RL baselines with predictive auxiliary targets across simulated environments, while improving overall performance on both realistic applications; (2) delivers up to $1.9\times$ and $2.0\times$ higher sample efficiency than ECHO and GRPO, respectively, on ScienceWorld; and (3) achieves these gains with a single policy backbone, without additional models, separate pretraining, or inference overhead. The codebase is available at https://github.com/ulab-uiuc/CoPE.

Learning to Steer LLM Agents and Multi-Agent Systems
Authors: Leyang Shen, Yaxin Gong, Yang Zhang, Chenxiao Fan, Jiakai Wang, Dong Wang, Yang Liu, Fuli Feng, Chun Kai Ling, Tat-Seng Chua

Abstract
Large language model (LLM)-based agents and multi-agent systems increasingly tackle complex tasks through long-horizon tool use and inter-agent collaboration. However, these systems are prone to deviating from the overall goal as execution unfolds, which wastes execution resources and leads to undesired outcomes, a failure termed goal drift. In this work, we propose a steering agent that supervises execution on the fly and steers the system back on track when drift occurs. The steering agent observes the execution context, deciding when to intervene with a low-cost fast path and how to intervene with a high-cost slow path. To unlock its steering intelligence, the ability to assess goal drift and generate effective interventions, we jointly train the two paths with reinforcement learning, combining a pairwise ranking objective with group-relative policy optimization (GRPO). Experiments on information-seeking search tasks show that the steering agent provides effective supervision for both single-agent and multi-agent systems, improving their performance and efficiency. The learned steering intelligence generalizes to out-of-domain tasks, transfers to unseen executors, and supports controllable intervention frequency without retraining.

Towards Continual Learning: Mitigating Forgetting With Subspace Tuning
Authors: Arnav Chavan, Kusha Sareen, Rishabh Tiwari, Abhranil Chandra, Johan Obando-Ceron, Kurt Keutzer, Scott Niekum, Inderjit S Dhillon, Devvrit Khatri
First: 2026-10-15T17:44:43+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:44:43+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) acquire new skills through fine-tuning, but it can destroy capabilities they already have: catastrophic forgetting remains a central obstacle to continual learning in LLMs. Recent works connect forgetting to how far, and along which directions, fine-tuning moves the weights. On-policy methods such as reinforcement learning and on-policy distillation avoid updating the principal singular directions of weight matrices and thus forget less. In contrast, SFT concentrates updates along these directions and is susceptible to catastrophic forgetting. In this work, we introduce Subspace Tuning (SST), which restricts each weight update to the bottom singular subspace, eliminating changes along the principal directions. SST is optimizer-agnostic and composes well with fine-tuning recipes including SFT and self-distillation. SST combines tight subspace protection, dense gradient estimation, and a simple refresh mechanism, enabling stronger learning than prior orthogonal fine-tuning methods. With these mechanisms, protecting as much as 99\% of the weight subspace substantially improves retention while preserving strong target-task performance. This behavior strengthens with model and data size and nearly eliminates forgetting in the large-model regime. SST also keeps the model close to its base distribution, inducing $2$--$4\times$ less KL drift than the baseline recipes. Overall, SST advances the learning--forgetting Pareto frontier, making it a practical default for fine-tuning pre-trained language models.

Learning Rational Strategies for Temporal Objectives in Multi-Agent Systems
Authors: Omar Adalat, Edwin Hamel-De le Court, Francesco Belardinelli
First: 2026-10-15T17:42:52+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:42:52+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
We study rational synthesis for stochastic multi-agent systems with unknown dynamics, where each agent is equipped with its own probabilistic temporal objective specified using Probabilistic Linear Temporal Logic on Finite Traces ($\mathsf{PLTL}_f}$). We impose solution concepts (e.g., Nash equilibrium, subgame-perfect equilibrium) as constraints on the joint strategy profile, using payoffs derived from each agent's probability of satisfying its temporal objective. In particular, we formulate per-agent rational synthesis as finding a strategy profile -- if one exists -- such that (i) the designated agent's probabilistic temporal constraint is satisfied, and (ii) the profile represents a stable solution under a chosen solution concept. We give conditional convergence guarantees for tabular Nash-Q learning, certify satisfaction with simultaneous finite-sample confidence bounds, and assess strategic stability with approximate best-response checking. Our approach operates in a stochastic \emph{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning} (MARL) environment, where model-free learning algorithms attempt to attain extremal satisfaction probabilities for temporal objectives automatically compiled into reward automata. Our approach is empirically validated using 8 general-sum concurrent stochastic games.

Abstract
Reward learning typically assumes the human's input covers everything that matters for the task. In practice, humans can only attend to so much at once, so their teaching naturally captures some features---or aspects of the task---better than others. Prior work has shown how the robot can identify which features were poorly supervised directly from its teaching data. But the current solution is to ask the human to try again, putting the burden on someone who already struggled to convey those features. We propose shifting this burden to the robot. Our key insight is that the same information that tells the robot what it doesn't know can help it ask better questions. Preference queries offer a low-effort channel for resolving this uncertainty, but only if the robot knows where to direct them and the human knows what to attend to when answering. We act on this insight in two ways: we build an informed prior that targets preference queries at the features that need resolving, and we accompany each query with an explanation that focuses the human's attention on exactly those features. In simulation, the informed prior beats the standard active preference learning baseline by up to $30.8\%$ in the low-query regime, where reducing human effort matters most. In a user study, the informed prior and explanations each significantly improve reward recovery and amplify each other.

Librarian Agents: Training LLMs to Curate Long-Context Workspaces that Anticipate Future Queries
Authors: Anikait Singh, Teresa Zhang, Yoonho Lee, Roshen Sanjay Nair, Aviral Kumar, Chelsea Finn

Abstract
Reasoning over long document contexts remains a challenge for large language models (LLMs). Standard retrieval methods are inherently reactive, bottlenecking agents with complex, cross-document reasoning at inference time, while precomputed structures like knowledge graphs often fall short because they rely on rigid heuristics. Instead, can we allow agents to perform anticipatory directed curation: learning to proactively reorganize unstructured corpora prior to inference to efficiently serve diverse future queries. We build on a key asymmetry in modern agentic harnesses (e.g., Claude Code): while LLM agents are adept at navigating existing filesystems to solve an immediate task, they are rarely trained to construct reusable workspaces for future use. To instantiate this capability, we introduce Librarian Agents, a framework in which a builder agent transforms raw documents into a hierarchical filesystem of synthetic files and reference links, which forms a workspace tailored for a downstream answering agent. We formulate workspace construction as a Markov decision process and optimize the builder policy via reinforcement learning to directly maximize downstream answering accuracy. Empirically, Librarian Agents outperforms the strongest retrieval and precomputation baselines by 33 percentage points on BrowseComp-Plus, amortizes upfront construction costs across repeated queries, and generalizes zero-shot to unseen domains on BRIGHT with a 31-point average accuracy improvement.

Minimizing Age of Incorrect Information in Pull-Based Remote Estimation of a Markov Source
Authors: Ismail Cosandal, Sennur Ulukus, Nail Akar
First: 2026-10-15T17:30:46+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:30:46+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
In this paper, we consider a discrete-time pull-based remote estimation system involving an information source and a remote monitor where the information source is a finite-state irreducible discrete-time Markov chain (DTMC) process. In this system, the remote monitor sends a pull request towards the source which responds by sampling its process so as to transmit the sampled values towards the monitor through a delay channel, encapsulated in information packets. We consider i) zero delay (ZD) and ii) unit delay (UD) channels, where the packet transmission times are zero and one slot, respectively. For remote estimation, we use the maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimator, and for quantifying its performance, we use the mean age of incorrect information (MAoII) which is a recently proposed freshness and mismatch metric that penalizes an incorrect estimation along with its duration. Our goal is to obtain pull policies so as to minimize MAoII under a long-run sampling rate constraint. For the ZD model, we propose a semi-Markov decision process (SMDP) formulation of the problem, which allows us to find the optimum observation-dependent pull policy. On the other hand, the UD channel prevents the monitor from possessing perfect information on the actual AoII process at any given time, and the SMDP formulation of the ZD channel cannot readily be used for the UD model. For the system with the UD channel, we first provide a numerical algorithm to calculate the MAoII and the average sampling rate for a given observation-dependent pull policy. For the optimization of the underlying remote estimation system, we use the joint distribution of the age and source processes to be obtained from the history of all observations, termed as belief. Subsequently, we propose a belief-dependent policy obtained via the belief-MDP formulation and a constrained deep reinforcement learning (CDRL) algorithm.

Baselines and Score Centering in Policy Gradient Estimation
Authors: Shuo Han, Ziqing Hu
First: 2026-10-15T17:28:50+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T17:28:50+00:00
Comments: 17 pages
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Reinforcement learning has become a widely used approach to post-training large language models. In a typical implementation, learning is off-policy: The policy generating the responses differs from the policy being trained because of training-inference mismatch or policy staleness in asynchronous training. This discrepancy is commonly addressed by importance sampling, whose ratio is usually regularized in practice through truncation, masking, or clipping. Under regularized importance sampling, the importance-weighted policy gradient is no longer invariant to constant shifts in the reward. This invariance is recovered by using the covariance between the reward and the importance-weighted score as the estimand, which coincides with the policy gradient when the ratio is not regularized. This note develops this covariance perspective for both sequence-level and token-level importance sampling. The covariance perspective reveals the roles of the group-mean baseline and score centering in policy gradient estimation. Both perform centering in covariance estimation, but they differ in the mean being subtracted: The group-mean baseline uses a sample mean, whereas score centering uses a population mean. The two lead to distinct unbiased covariance estimators with different variances. Whether both forms of centering should be used simultaneously depends on whether importance sampling is applied at the sequence level or at the token level. With sequence-level importance sampling, score centering adds no benefit once the group-mean baseline is used; with token-level importance sampling, using the group-mean baseline without score centering generally introduces bias when the importance ratio is regularized.

Reinforcement learning has become a widely used approach to post-training large language models.

Abstract
Chain-of-Thought (CoT) reasoning has significantly improved LLMs' mathematical problem-solving capabilities, but distilling such capabilities into smaller models remains challenging due to the capacity mismatch between verbose teachers and compact students. Directly copying teachers' lengthy reasoning chains causes capacity overload, resulting in truncated outputs or repetitive failure. Existing remedies each sacrifice a critical property of CoT: implicit reasoning methods (e.g., compressing reasoning into hidden states) trade away interpretability and verifiability, while heuristic compression strategies (e.g., random step pruning) destroy logical integrity. To address this, we propose BRIDGE, a curriculum framework that first establishes structural understanding via masked reconstruction, then uses GRPO-based reinforcement learning to guide students in self-discovering the optimal balance between accuracy and brevity, and finally internalizes complex reasoning through teacher-guided rewriting on failure cases. On GSM8K, BRIDGE enables Qwen2.5-3B to achieve 11.29% accuracy improvement and 27.4% token reduction over the original model, outperforming instruction-tuned variants and distillation baselines. Zero-shot transfer experiments on SVAMP and MATH-500 further confirm the generalization of internalized reasoning. Our code and model checkpoints are publicly available at https://github.com/Applied-Machine-Learning-Lab/SDM2026_BRIDGE and https://huggingface.co/bowen0815/BRIDGE.

Abstract
Accurate motion prediction is critical for safe autonomous driving, yet even strong forecasters leave residual errors in their multimodal proposals. Existing refiners compress complete proposals or update their segments sequentially, preventing the full proposed future from serving as explicit relational context for correction. Abstract

Abstract
Approximate multipliers can reduce hardware area and energy consumption in Deep Neural Network (DNN) inference; however, they introduce computational errors. Assessing the accuracy of numerous approximate multiplier designs across diverse DNN models and large-scale datasets remains challenging due to prohibitive evaluation times. This overhead primarily stems from the slow emulation of approximate multiplier behavior using look-up tables (LUTs) on CPU and GPU platforms. Moreover, the resulting accuracy degradation must be carefully quantified and, if necessary, mitigated (e.g., through retraining), further increasing the overall evaluation cost. To address these challenges, we propose FAME, an FPGA-based platform for evaluating approximate multipliers. The platform exploits the reconfigurable logic of Field-Programmable Gate Arrays (FPGAs) to implement approximate multipliers directly in hardware, eliminating the need for LUT-based emulation on CPU/GPU platforms and thereby enabling efficient DNN inference while significantly reducing evaluation time on large datasets. Furthermore, we introduce a pattern-guided DNN retraining technique to mitigate accuracy degradation induced by approximate multipliers. Specifically, retraining is guided by multiplier-specific patterns to effectively recover potential accuracy losses. We evaluate FAME using two DNN models, ResNet-18 and MobileNetV2, on the ImageNet dataset across 27 approximate multipliers. During inference, our approach achieves up to a 3.47x speedup in approximate multiplier evaluation compared to prior LUT-based emulation methods. Furthermore, the proposed retraining technique improves accuracy by up to 65.5% over existing retraining approaches for the evaluated multipliers. Attention over noisy examples: generalization and memorization in the exponential Hopfield network
Authors: Francesco Alemanno
First: 2026-10-15T16:43:55+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-15T16:43:55+00:00
Comments: 15 pages, 9 figures. Code, data and Monte Carlo results at https://github.com/francescoalemanno/hebb-attention
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
In Hebbian learning from examples a Hopfield network of $N$ neurons is not supplied with the $K$ archetypes it has to retrieve but with $M$ blurred examples of each, of quality $r$, and whether it can infer them depends on the load $α=K/N$ (interference among them) and on the dataset entropy $ρ=(1-r^2)/(Mr^2)$ (how little the examples say about them). We transfer this protocol to the exponential Hopfield model, whose zero-temperature update is a step of softmax attention with the unlabelled examples as keys. A signal-to-noise analysis gives in closed form the overlap between the output and an unseen archetype, $m^{(1)}={\rm erf}(1/\sqrt{2ρ_{\rm eff}})$: the majority-rule law of the pairwise model at small load, with $ρ$ replaced by an attention entropy $ρ_{\rm eff}$ built from the weight the softmax gives to the correct class and the number of examples it spreads it over. Two consequences follow. First, in a window of inverse temperatures $β$ that widens as $\sqrt N$ the interference between archetypes is exponentially small in $Nβ$: the load drops out and the number of archetypes that can be learnt grows as $\exp(c\sqrt N)$. Second, learning fails differently: in the pairwise model through a spin-glass phase, in the exponential model through memorization: the softmax concentrates its weight on the training example closest to the input and returns it in place of the archetype. Seen from the input the softmax weights are those of the random energy model, and memorization is its freezing transition, at $β\sqrt{N(1-r^2)}\simeq\sqrt{2\ln M}$; it is measurable on a head whose keys are examples as the number of examples carrying weight. On MNIST the diagnostic follows the random-energy prediction; on fashion-MNIST it detects sub-clusters in the classes, and the best accuracy lies beyond the memorization threshold, where the softmax reads out a local sub-prototype.

Abstract
Most existing agentic reinforcement learning methods assume single-session trajectories, where interaction proceeds within one continuously accumulated context. However, agentic techniques such as context compaction and subagent forking may produce trajectories spanning multiple sessions, creating a mismatch between the single-session training assumption and inference-time execution. Therefore, we introduce Multi-Session Agentic Reinforcement Learning, a more general setting that supports an arbitrary number of sessions within a trajectory. Under this setting, a widely adopted method is to broadcast trajectory-level advantages to all sessions, but this does not distinguish contributions from various sessions or guide session allocation within trajectories. To address this issue, we propose Mosaic, a multi-session agentic RL method with critic-free hierarchical optimization. At the trajectory level, it incorporates session-usage signals into advantage estimation. At the session level, it shapes each session's advantage according to the estimated marginal contribution. Experiments on BrowseComp with Qwen3.5-4B show that Mosaic outperforms broadcasting in both context compaction and subagent forking scenarios while using fewer sessions on average. Further analyses show that our method achieves improvements in session utility and allocation, which we suggested is a promising target for future advances in multi-session agentic RL.

Abstract
Reinforcement learning agents optimize exactly the reward signal they are given, which can diverge sharply from an intended true objective when that signal is only a proxy for it; this failure mode is known as specification gaming. Existing evidence for this failure mode is largely observational, with comparatively few demonstrations pairing a controlled, deliberately constructed proxy/true gap with a mitigation that carries a verifiable guarantee rather than only an empirical improvement. We construct a minimal gridworld in which a cleaning-robot agent can either genuinely remove dirt or conceal it from a cheap sensor, and show that a standard deep Q-network trained only on the sensor's proxy reward converges to a policy that games it completely, reaching near-maximal proxy reward while never achieving the true objective. Abstract
