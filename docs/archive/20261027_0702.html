RobustRLlib: A Unified Library and Benchmark for Robust Reinforcement Learning Algorithms
Authors: Xi Chen, Fengqi Hu, Zeyu Shen, Jiaying Xu, Shangding Gu, Pan Xu, Anqi Liu, Laixi Shi
First: 2026-10-23T17:57:22+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T17:57:22+00:00
Comments: Project page, code, and tutorial: https://robustrl-lib.github.io/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Robust reinforcement learning (RL) is critical for reliable real-world deployment, where policies may fail under diverse shifts and disruptions. Existing robust RL algorithms offer a rich set of mechanisms and principles, yet two major barriers limit their broader potential: limited algorithm reusability and an unclear empirical capability boundary. We introduce RobustRLlib, an algorithm-centric library and benchmark that addresses both challenges. It integrates 17 representative robust online, offline, and safe RL algorithms under a unified interface for substantially improved reusability, together with an enhanced evaluation platform spanning diverse real-world shifts and advanced humanoid and vision-language-action tasks. Systematic evaluation over 273 perturbation configurations provides an up-to-date capability map of current robust RL algorithms, revealing where they remain effective and where substantial robustness gaps persist. As an open-source, user-friendly platform, RobustRLlib offers a reproducible and extensible codebase for applying existing methods to new problems and developing new robust RL algorithms.

DexPort: A Hand-Held Skill Interface for Dexterous Hand Data Collection
Authors: Amber Xie, Jiawei Fu, Xiaofeng Guo, Carmelo Sferrazza, Guanya Shi, Haozhi Qi
First: 2026-10-23T17:56:28+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T17:56:28+00:00
Comments: Website: https://amberxie88.github.io/dexport/
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Collecting high-quality demonstrations remains a central bottleneck for learning dexterous manipulation. Teleoperation records executable robot actions, but it requires a complete robot setup and places the burden of coordinating many finger joints on the operator. Human demonstrations are easier to collect, but their motions must be estimated and retargeted to the robot, and they do not directly provide robot actions. In this paper, we introduce DexPort, a portable data-collection interface for high-DoF robot hands. It uses the same hand hardware for collection and deployment, avoiding a hand embodiment gap, and records both commanded joint targets and measured joint states. Our key observation is that a small set of skills can support a wide range of dexterous tasks. During collection, the operator moves the hand-held interface to specify the robot wrist motion and selects or adjusts skills that coordinate the finger motion. This removes the need to control each joint independently while retaining behaviors such as precise grasping and in-hand manipulation. We collect demonstrations across several real-world tasks and in unstructured environments without a robot arm. We then use behavior cloning to train visuomotor policies directly on the recorded wrist motions and hand commands, without skill labels. We evaluate across 4 tasks: pick-and-place with diverse objects, screwing with in-hand tool use, drawer stowage which requires simultaneous grasp and pulling, and a bimanual scanning task. We collect over 2,500 demonstrations with our portable device, in both laboratory and in-the-wild settings. Across our tasks, we see an improvement from 11% to 64% when using recorded robot commands instead of states, and an improvement from 36% to 78% for task success when using in-the-wild data.

Abstract
Video retrieval at scale is central to data curation and safety validation in autonomous driving, where users want to find not only scenes but also dynamic events such as cut-ins and hard braking. Existing vision-language and keyword-based retrieval methods often miss these events because the relevant motion may not be explicitly described in text or captured by lexical overlap. Rule-based retrieval can encode such events more directly, but it is brittle: generated or hand-written rules often fail when their assumptions do not match real driving data. We propose STRIVE-D, a data-calibrated retrieval framework for driving videos. It uses weakly labeled in-domain videos to estimate when a query rule is reliable, adapt rules that mismatch observed data, and fuse calibrated rule scores with vision-language and keyword-based retrieval signals. Across three driving benchmarks, including newly released human-annotated event data on DrivingDojo, STRIVE-D delivers up to 84% relative improvement in top-1 accuracy over state-of-the-art methods.

Abstract
For robots to work reliably in commercial and industrial applications, recent advances in agentic coding systems have potential to combine interpretable robot programming with the open-world adaptability of model-free policies. We focus on "Variational Automation" (VA), a class of tasks that have larger variations in object geometry and pose than fixed automation. Where Rounding Happens Matters: Rounding-Location Alignment Stabilizes LLM Reinforcement Learning

Abstract
Reinforcement learning for large language models often uses separate systems for inference and training. Even with identical weights and BF16 tensors, implementation differences can make an intended on-policy update off-policy and destabilize training. Existing remedies align the entire inference and training stacks, change global precision, or modify the learning algorithm. We ask whether aligning selected parts of the execution stack can stabilize BF16 RL while other implementation differences remain. We align training-side rounding locations with inference in three regions---RoPE, SwiGLU, and residual RMSNorm---by retaining selected intermediates in FP32 until their BF16 outputs. Across four Qwen-family models, aligning all three regions prevents the collapse observed under the default BF16 baseline; RoPE-path alignment alone also suffices in the studied runs, despite remaining numerical discrepancies. The interventions keep the inference engine, matrix-multiplication and attention kernels, and learning objective unchanged.

If at First You Don't Succeed, Backtrack: Lightweight Recovery for Frozen VLAs
Authors: Thomas Landeg, Thierry Tambe
First: 2026-10-23T17:35:33+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T17:35:33+00:00
Comments: 17 pages, 1 figure, 14 tables

Abstract
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models perform well on robotic manipulation, yet remain brittle once their own actions carry them into states their training data barely covers. Recovering from such failures usually requires recovery demonstrations, policy retraining, or a separate model at inference time. We introduce Backtrack, a lightweight failure detection and recovery mechanism for frozen VLAs that needs none of these. A small failure prediction head, trained only on outcome-labeled rollouts of the frozen policy, reads the VLA's existing internal representations to estimate whether the current episode will fail. When the predicted failure probability exceeds a threshold, a controller pauses the policy, retraces the arm's recently achieved motion, and hands control back to the unchanged policy so it can retry from an earlier configuration. The head adds only 0.48-0.88% to the policy's parameter count. We evaluate Backtrack on LIBERO with three VLAs spanning two action-generation families: SmolVLA and $π$0.5, which use flow-matching action experts, and the autoregressive $π$0-FAST. On LIBERO-Long, the suite of long-horizon multi-step tasks and the one on which all three policies fail most often, Backtrack reduces each policy's failure rate by 19-48%, raising success by up to 7.4 percentage points; on the three shorter suites, where control success is already 85-99%, its effect is smaller. On MetaWorld, all twelve controller configurations tested with SmolVLA improve on the unassisted policy.

Authors: Danilo Vucetic, Alexandre Larouche, Mohammad Pedramfar, Audrey Durand, Gauthier Gidel

Abstract
Regularised reinforcement learning (RL) algorithms rely on estimators, like Monte Carlo tree search or one-step look-ahead, to provide policy, value, or Q-value estimates in training and inference. However, existing bandit-based estimation frameworks evaluate sampling efficiency, not the quality of estimates. We address this by formalising estimate generation as a recommendation process, treating bandit-based sampling as an improvement operator. To evaluate the quality of a recommendation (estimate), we define regulariser-aware recommender regrets, analysing their lower bounds, and proving that simple algorithms like uniform sampling can achieve these bounds. Finally, we unify bandit- and search-based target improvement under recommender MCTS. Across synthetic trees, arcade games, and scientific discovery tasks, recommenders often yield faster target convergence and superior downstream performance. Our experiments characterise for the first time the potential of regularised MCTS in scientific applications.

Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies can adapt to local human intervention, while federated learning can combine distributed robot experience without pooling raw records. We examine their prospective intersection: sharing adaptations derived from diverse human corrections across tasks, environments, people, and robot embodiments. Corrections differ in modality, safety semantics(urgency, behavioral scope, etc.), and privacy sensitivity. A shared update that improves mean task success may suppress a rare safety intervention or produce an unsafe response on another robot. We argue for three requirements: preserve the context and uncertainty of multimodal corrections when deciding what to share; assess candidate updates in each receiving robot's closed loop before deployment; and evaluate leakage alongside retention of safety-critical evidence. Immediate, legible defensive behavior remains necessary during human interaction. We offer counterexamples and a falsifiable evaluation agenda, not experimental results.

Reinforcement Learning with Multi-Step Transition Predictions: A Bellman Theory
Authors: Chenbei Lu, Zaiwei Chen, Tongxin Li, Chenye Wu, Adam Wierman
First: 2026-10-23T16:55:37+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T16:55:37+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Multi-step predictions of future transition realizations can improve reinforcement learning, but their value remains difficult to quantify. Directly including these predictions in the state can lead to exponential state-space growth, making direct dynamic programming computationally expensive. We study discounted finite MDPs under a block-feedback protocol: at the start of each K-period block, the decision maker observes $K$-step probabilistic transition predictions for a subset of actions and adapts decisions to observed states within the block. We show that the optimal value satisfies a Bayesian Bellman equation on the original state space. This representation reveals that predictive information creates value by recursively changing the local order of maximization and expectation in the Bellman expansion. We formalize this effect through a Bellman--Jensen analysis and quantify the effects of prediction horizon, error, action coverage, and within-block feedback. We propose block-feedback Bayesian offline learning with online adaptation (BF--BOLA), a model-based algorithm for learning near-optimal policies, and establish upper and lower sample-complexity bounds that match up to logarithmic factors in the stated regimes. The results show that predictions can reduce transition learning to uncovered rows and that learning a near-optimal policy can require fewer prediction samples than estimating the Bayesian value function.

Abstract
Fine-tuning on datasets of unknown origin can create a backdoor in the trained model when the data includes poisoned examples. Difficult to localise and remove, backdoors can persist through safety training. While parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods capture the full parameter changes introduced during training, they are not easily interpretable. We present the full method of TopKLoRA, extending the preliminary proposal of Masiak et al. (2025), an interpretable-by-design LoRA variant in which only the $k$ largest adapter latents are active at each token. Unlike SAEs, TopKLoRA is trained purely on downstream loss, where optimisation pressure incentivises learning functionally causal latents. We demonstrate this sparse LoRA adapter in a sleeper-agent backdoor case study where we create model organisms by fine-tuning gemma-2-2b, Qwen2.5-1.5B, and Qwen2.5-7B on a poisoned instruction-following dataset. For each organism, we identify and ablate a circuit that is both necessary and sufficient to implement the backdoor. Furthermore, our new fine-tuning method produces sparse circuits that are up to an order of magnitude smaller than a dense LoRA baseline, allowing surgical ablation of the malicious capability without sacrificing instruction-following performance. Finally, we propose a technique to construct model organisms with localised circuits using TopKLoRA latents and gradient-routing, which we use to validate the findings of our circuit search algorithm. We consider these findings as a step toward interpretable-by-design architectures that provide faithful units of analysis without compromising model performance. We share our code: https://github.com/interpretable-finetuning/TopKLoRA.

Abstract
Regularised reinforcement learning (RL) has provable robustness guarantees that should ease the transfer of policies from simulation to robots, known as sim-to-real. Yet, weakly regularised algorithms like Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) remain the de facto choice in sim-to-real pipelines, while the practical benefits of regularisation for transfer remain poorly characterised. In this work, we study the sim-to-real performance of commonly used RL algorithms, with the aim of characterising the effects of regularisation on robotic performance. To that end, we derive Soft MellowMax Actor-Critic (SMM-AC), a novel regularised RL algorithm based on the soft mellowmax value function, which carries strong robustness guarantees. On MuJoCo continuous control benchmarks, SMM-AC is competitive with baselines like Soft Actor-Critic (SAC). On real robots, SMM-AC achieves higher return, while exhibiting greater robustness to perturbations in observations, the environment, and dynamics than baselines. Together, these results demonstrate that regularised RL improves real-world policy deployment through better robustness to the sim-to-real gap, and changing real-world conditions.

Abstract
Timely state updates are essential for coordination and monitoring in multi-agent systems, such as teams of mobile robots exchanging state information. When agents share a wireless channel, collisions and slow convergence of distributed access schemes can degrade information freshness over finite operating periods. This paper proposes age-threshold frame-based random access (AFRA), a distributed medium access control protocol for minimizing age of information (AoI). Once its age reaches a threshold, an agent starts a local frame with a mandatory transmission and, if unsuccessful, makes one further attempt at a uniformly selected remaining slot. Each attempt carries a freshly generated update. AFRA requires only local state and acknowledgment feedback, with shared slot boundaries but independently timed local frames. We prove almost-sure convergence from any legal initial state to collision-free periodic operation when the frame length $L$ and age threshold $T$ satisfy $L\ge T>N\ge2$, where $N$ is the number of agents. We also derive closed-form expressions for steady-state throughput, average AoI, and average peak AoI. Simulations demonstrate that AFRA combines rapid convergence at small age thresholds with lower window-averaged AoI than the existing age-aware random access schemes evaluated in this study.

Abstract

Thus, the aim of this work was to explore whether HER2 status can be predicted directly from whole-brain post-contrast T1-weighted (T1w) MRI scans in breast cancer brain metastases (BCBM) using deep learning, and whether providing explicit tumour segmentation information improves prediction. DT-JEPA: Learning to Predict Future Representations for Decision-Making
Authors: Changxu Ni, Yiyang Ni, Jun Li, Feiran You, Hongyang Du, Haitao Zhao, Shi Jin
First: 2026-10-23T14:04:18+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-23T14:04:18+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Decision Transformer (DT) has demonstrated strong performance in various offline reinforcement learning (RL) tasks by conditioning action generation on expected future returns and historical trajectories. However, treating expected future returns as the condition provides an incomplete description of the future, as trajectories with different state transitions and behaviors may share similar returns. Moreover, expected future returns inherently depend on task-specific reward functions, which makes policy learning reliant on reward-labeled datasets and restricts its scalability to tasks with similar but unseen rewards. Recently, Joint Embedding Predictive Architectures (JEPA) have emerged as a promising paradigm of self-supervised learning-based world models, which learn predictive representations of future dynamics in latent space rather than reconstructing future states in the original space. Inspired by JEPA, we propose DT-JEPA, an offline pretraining and online fine-tuning framework that learns general knowledge from reward-free offline trajectories and achieves excellent adaptation to unseen tasks. Instead of predicting a single deterministic future embedding, we design a novel probabilistic predictor to model the conditional distribution of future representations in the latent space, thereby capturing both predictive information and uncertainty about future dynamics. Theoretically, we show that 1) the JEPA objective seeks to maximize a variational lower bound on the mutual information between historical representations and future trajectories; and 2) predictive future-conditioned policy learning admits a finite-sample performance bound with an O(N-1/4) estimation term. Experiments on D4RL benchmarks demonstrate that DT-JEPA achieves efficient adaptation across varying offline RL tasks, outperforming traditional DT-based and other offline RL methods.

Abstract
Balancing exploration with exploitation is a core challenge in black-box optimization and reinforcement learning. We introduce POETS ($\textbf{Po}$licy $\textbf{E}$nsembles for $\textbf{T}$hompson $\textbf{S}$ampling), a novel framework that bridges uncertainty quantification and policy optimization. Our approach is grounded in the insight that policies trained with Kullback-Leibler (KL) regularization implicitly encode an underlying reward function. Building on this, POETS bypasses the complex, nested process of training an uncertainty-aware reward model and separately fitting a policy to this model. Instead, we directly train a policy ensemble to capture epistemic uncertainty by matching implicitly encoded reward functions to online, bootstrapped data. To overcome the prohibitive compute and memory constraints of ensembling Large Language Models (LLMs), POETS utilizes an efficient architecture: the ensemble shares a pre-trained backbone while maintaining diversity through independent Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) branches. Theoretically, we show that POETS implicitly approximates KL-regularized Thompson sampling, a principled optimization strategy with optimal cumulative regret bounds of $O(\sqrt{T γ_T})$. Empirically, we demonstrate that POETS achieves state-of-the-art sample efficiency across diverse scientific discovery domains, including protein search, therapeutic molecular design, and quantum circuit design. POETS also improves the optimization trajectory and out-of-distribution transfer of reinforcement learning across mathematical reasoning and code generation tasks, proving particularly robust in off-policy settings with experience replay or in small dataset regimes.

Authors: SungJae Ahn, Jeong Woon Lee, Kyoleen Kwak, Hyoseok Hwang

Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Project1
Abstract
Deep Reinforcement Learning policies can produce nonsmooth action oscillations that hinder deployment on physical robots. Existing architectural and penalty-based approaches seek spatial smoothness by directly reducing sensitivity to changes in state inputs, but their broad constraints can degrade task performance as stronger smoothing is pursued. Temporal regularization instead constrains action differences along observed transitions, but has been considered unable to provide the spatial smoothness needed under observation noise. We revisit this assumption by proving that the temporal penalty bounds the expected action differences between current states sharing a next state, revealing a spatial effect that empirically extends to spatial smoothness. Building on this finding, we propose Conditioning for Action using only Temporal Smoothness (CATS), which combines a temporal penalty with linear ramp-up. We highlight temporal regularization's ability to provide spatial smoothness while better preserving task performance than explicit spatial regularization. Through linear ramp-up, CATS allows the policy to learn rewarding behavior before progressively smoothing its actions, improving return preservation and both temporal and spatial smoothness. Experiments in both simulation and the real world show that CATS substantially reduces action oscillation without degrading task performance, with little computational overhead.

Random jump forests are introduced as a nonparametric tool for estimating conditional cumulative transition rates and occupation probabilities in multi-state models. The main idea is that a single tree partitions the covariate space for all transitions simultaneously, using partially observed trajectories, and with Nelson--Aalen estimators at the terminal nodes. Occupation probabilities are recovered through the product integral without requiring the Markov assumption. Uniform consistency and a functional central limit theorem for the estimators of cumulative rates can be established under the assumption that the covariate vector takes finitely many values, and under suitable splitting conditions. In particular, pointwise confidence intervals and simultaneous bands are available in the Markov case. Simulation studies show a predictive accuracy close to the correctly specified benchmark models and marked improvements when benchmarks are misspecified, with further illustration on four biomedical data sets. In the coverage study, confidence bands attain near-nominal coverage in several settings, with pooling of heterogeneous rates being the main source of possible undercoverage. The method, including prediction assessment and variable importance, is implemented in the R package JumpForests.

Abstract
Trajectory transfer from rollout to training affects both learning quality and system efficiency in large language model reinforcement learning (RL). Enforcing batch-wide training requirements can delay the admission of completed trajectories, resulting in higher architectural staleness: the additional parameter-version gap from disaggregated pipelining relative to colocated execution. Abstract
Post-training quantization (PTQ) to FP4 has emerged as a key technique for reducing large language model inference cost, especially on Blackwell GPUs, which offer native FP4 tensor core support. Direct PTQ to FP4, however, still leaves a noticeable accuracy gap. Recent work on SVDQuant mitigates the errors introduced through FP4 quantization for diffusion models by adding high-precision low-rank corrections obtained via SVD. However, this high-precision low-rank correction requires many bits per rank, and it runs on the same tensor cores as the main FP4 GEMM. With batch size $1$, the fused kernel is at most $11\%$ slower than the FP4-only GEMM for every rank up to $512$. At rank $128$, it is at most $20\%$ slower up to batch size $8$.
