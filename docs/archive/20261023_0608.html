LongZero: Enhancing Long-Context Reasoning in LLMs from Zero Data
Authors: Zhennan Wan, Jianfei Chen
First: 2026-10-21T17:59:14+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:59:14+00:00
Comments: Code and data are available at https://github.com/zhennan1/LongZero
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) is an effective way to strengthen the long-context reasoning of large language models (LLMs), but it needs training data that are long, diverse, challenging, and verifiable. Human annotation of such data is expensive, naturally occurring data are too scarce to sustain RL, and existing synthesis methods depend on external corpora or stronger teacher models, which become harder to obtain as the trained models get stronger. Recently, RL from zero data, in which a model learns from tasks it proposes itself, has achieved excellent performance, but existing methods target short-context tasks. Therefore, we propose $\textbf{LongZero}$, a method that lets an LLM improve its own long-context reasoning without any external data. Instead of writing questions and answers over existing documents, LongZero first has the model synthesize the question and answer with its supporting evidence in a compact structured form, and only then separately expands the evidence into a long multi-document context. LongZero combines these self-written multi-hop reasoning tasks with self-written multi-round retrieval tasks and trains the model with GRPO. We evaluate LongZero on two LLMs across five long-context reasoning benchmarks and compare it with RL on data synthesized from external corpora. Experimental results show that LongZero can generalize to real tasks, and outperforms both baselines, suggesting a viable and promising path to improving long-context reasoning of LLMs.

Asymmetric On-Policy Distillation Learns In-Context Exploration
Authors: Sriyash Poddar, Yanda Bao, Jacob Krantz, Matthew Chang, Xavier Puig, Roozbeh Mottaghi, Natasha Jaques, Abhishek Gupta
First: 2026-10-21T17:50:31+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:50:31+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Sequential decision-making under partial observability is difficult because agents must explore to infer hidden, task-relevant information before acting effectively. Learning such information-gathering behaviors tabula rasa via online reinforcement learning is sample-inefficient, whereas offline imitation averages expert actions across hidden states and fails to learn exploration. In this work, we propose ASTEROID, a simple and effective student--teacher supervised learning framework that can learn test-time exploration. Although a privileged expert never needs to explore, we demonstrate that a history-conditioned student can learn to do so when trained with on-policy contexts: the student first collects on-policy context under partial observability, and the privileged expert provides action labels conditioned on that context. By distilling expert actions directly conditioned on the student's on-policy history, the student learns to systematically resolve partial observability, i.e., search while uncertain and exploit once the hidden state is revealed. This enables efficient test-time exploration (similar to Bayesian posterior sampling) without incurring the impractical training-data costs of reinforcement learning. Across a diverse range of eleven different environments, including discrete exploration benchmarks, robotic manipulation, navigation, and Procgen environments, ASTEROID achieves ~2x higher returns under fixed exploration budgets, uses ~100x fewer interactions than RL baselines, and outperforms frontier models at exploration. Finally, our method transfers policies trained in simulation to vision-denied real-world robot manipulation, exhibiting efficient exploration and increasing success by +40% over standard distillation. Overall, it provides a simple yet surprisingly effective framework to learn efficient test-time exploration.

Differentially Private Sparse Reward Estimation with Preference Feedback
Authors: Meng Ding, Mingxi Lei, Jie Zhang, Jinyan Liu, Di Wang
First: 2026-10-21T17:45:42+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:45:42+00:00
Comments: 36 pages
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Reinforcement Learning with Human Feedback (RLHF) has become a central paradigm for aligning Large Language Models with human values. However, the reliance on sensitive preference data necessitates rigorous differential privacy (DP) guarantees to prevent user information leakage. Existing private alignment methods typically assume dense reward parameterizations, resulting in estimation error bounds that scale polynomially with the ambient {model size $d$}. Such dependence is prohibitive in modern {overparameterized models} where $d$ is vast. In this work, we study \emph{Private Sparse RLHF} problem to address this limitation, assuming that human preferences (reward model) are governed by a subset of $s^* \ll d$ relevant features. We provide the first comprehensive theoretical framework for differentially private sparse reward estimation from pairwise preference feedback under the Bradley-Terry-Luce model, and introduce efficient algorithms for both Sample-DP and Label-DP settings. Theoretically, under the squared $\ell_2$ parameter estimation error, we establish upper bounds of $\widetilde{O}(s^*/n + {s^*}^2/n^2\varepsilon^2)$ for $(\varepsilon,δ)$-Sample-DP and $\widetilde{O}(s^*/n + s^*/n\varepsilon^2)$ for $\varepsilon$-Label-DP based on randomized response, where $n$ is the training data size. For the lower bounds, we show that the Sample-DP rate is nearly optimal, and that the guarantee for our Label-DP method cannot be further improved within the randomized-response framework. We also provide a general lower bound for the problem with Label-DP. Empirically, our framework demonstrates superior efficacy over dense baselines across sentiment generation and safety alignment benchmarks, offering a significantly more favorable privacy-utility trade-off.

From Classifier Heads to Encoder Prototypes: Personalized Federated Learning with Head-Derived Clustering
Authors: Amirardalan Dehghanpour, Arian Noori, Ehsan Tanghatari, Naimeh Omidvar, Hamid R. Rabiee
First: 2026-10-21T17:27:46+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:27:46+00:00

Abstract
Personalized federated learning must decide which clients should share representations. We introduce HDC-FL (Head-Derived Clustering for Personalized Federated Learning), which reads that decision off a signal shared-encoder training already produces: the client-specific classifi- er heads. At periodic refresh rounds the server projects the cached heads into a normalized low-rank descriptor space, groups the descriptors, builds one encoder prototype per group from the cached client encoders, and returns to each client a soft mixture of those prototypes paired with its own unchanged head. Client-specific heads are never averaged or distributed to oth- er clients; the shared-encoder trajectory is left intact; and no second optimization trajectory, additional local objective, or post-training fine-tuning stage is required. Across 11 CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 configurations covering label-subset, Dirichlet and rotation heterogeneity with ResNet-18 and MobileNetV2 encoders, HDC-FL is competitive with the strongest personalized and clustered baselines in macro-averaged mean accuracy and attains the numerically highest macro-averaged worst-client and bottom-20% accuracy among the evaluated methods. The tail results distinguish methods that look similar when only their means are compared. A controlled donor-swap diagnostic further finds positive donor-ranking associations between head similarity and encoder compatibility in all three settings tested, most clearly under covariate shift.

Fast Cascaded Recursive Filtering via a Block-Matrix Reformulation
Authors: Haotian Zhai, Bernd-Peter Paris
First: 2026-07-15T17:24:52+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:26:17+00:00
Comments: 13 pages, 8 figures
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Recursive (IIR) filters realized as cascaded second-order sections (biquads) offer both design generality and robustness against coefficient quantization. However, their inherent sample-to-sample feedback dependency poses a fundamental obstacle to parallel computation. This paper reformulates the biquad difference equation as a banded block-Toeplitz linear system. It relies on a stride-$N$ permutation that maps a group of $NL$ samples into a block-tridiagonal structure whose entries are scalar multiples of identity and shift matrices. Within this framework, two data-parallel algorithms are developed which, to the best of our knowledge, are the fastest, single-core IIR filter implementations reported to date. A partial LU (PH) factorization preserves the sparse block structure and a cyclic reduction reduces the sequential dependency depth from $\mathcal{O}(N)$ to $\mathcal{O}(\log_2 N)$. For a cascade of $K$ biquads, the intermediate permutations between successive sections cancel exactly, so that only a single pair of permutations is required for the entire cascade. Exact block-level operation counts are derived for every algorithmic stage and validated against cycle-accurate measurements on three micro-architectures supporting AVX2 SIMD instructions. Experimental results for a 16th-order system show that the proposed multi-block algorithms reduce clock cycles per sample by up to $10\times$ compared to scalar filtering, with both algorithms scaling favorably on newer architectures. On a single Meteor Lake core, cyclic reduction achieves approximately 616 MS/s---four times the throughput of Intel's vendor-tuned IPP library.

Does Centralized Learning Help Embodied Agents Coordinate, and When Can a Tabular Study Tell?
Authors: Muhammad Ahmed Atif, Nehal Naeem Haji, Muhammad Ebad Atif, Mohammad Shahid Shaikh
First: 2026-01-24T13:09:01+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T17:09:52+00:00
Comments: 10 pages, 2 figures. Under peer review at AAMAS 2027
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Tabular studies are a common way to compare independent and centralized multi-agent learners, because they avoid function approximation. A natural expectation is that centralized learning, which learns joint actions, helps agents coordinate, especially when their bodies make solo success hard. Earlier work tests this only in parts: it measures coordination for one learning method, compares methods with the agents' bodies fixed, or varies speed but reports only how well the team did. We test it in a predator-prey environment, where teammates cooperatively hunt together while two teams compete. We compare independent Q-learning (IQL) with central Q-learning (CQL) in all four predator and prey pairings, under limits on speed and stamina, on a 5x5 and an 8x8 grid trained for 200,000 episodes each, and check that the agents learn on both. We measure coordination directly, with predictions for the main measurement fixed in advance. In this task, where one predator can capture prey alone, we find three things. First, CQL costs its agents in both roles on both grids, and its predators coordinate no more than IQL's. Second, its one benefit comes from acting on the best joint action: this cut capture time by 42% on the small grid with faster prey and unlimited stamina, but helped nowhere on the larger grid, where the best joint action had mostly never been tried. Third, on the larger grid a tie-breaking default decided which method won, even though the agents had passed a learning check. A tabular comparison can therefore be trusted only if it also checks how much of the state space the agents covered and implements the methods alike. We propose simple checks for both.

To Retain or to Adapt? Generalizing Continual Learning
Authors: Giulia Lanzillotta, Mandana Samiei, Doina Precup, Razvan Pascanu, Claire Vernade
Venue: Transactions on Machine Learning Research, 2026
First: 2026-07-06T20:11:53+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T16:55:11+00:00
Comments: https://openreview.net/forum?id=C4zRmT9jYO
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
The Continual Learning (CL) literature has long been driven by the goal of mitigating catastrophic forgetting. This objective rests on an often unstated assumption: that a lifelong learner should approximate the Joint-Task Learning (JTL) solution and retain all previously acquired knowledge. We challenge this retention-centered premise, arguing that in non-stationary environments prioritizing retention can impede real-time adaptation. Shifting the focus to the Average Lifelong Error (ALE), we formalize CL as an online optimization problem governed by the interaction between environmental and learning dynamics. We introduce Transfer Efficiency as a quantitative measure of the tension between Instability, the bias inherited from conflicting past experience, and Transient Error, the optimization cost of learning new tasks from scratch. Under mild convergence conditions, holding across linear and neural network models, this decomposition yields a Critical Task Duration: a closed-form threshold beyond which historical knowledge transitions from a warm-start advantage to an optimization liability whenever retention induces a positive stationary bias. We validate these theoretical predictions on continual image classification and reinforcement learning benchmarks. Finally, by connecting continual learning to the online learning framework of predictable sequences, we show that JTL is only one instance of a broader family of objectives, and we propose a new general class of continual learning algorithms, which we call Predictive Continual Learning. Predictive CL algorithms optimize expected future performance under an explicit, dynamically updated model of future tasks. As a proof of concept, we analyze a Window algorithm that interpolates between JTL and Independent-Task Learning (ITL), outperforming both under controlled distributional drift.

The Continual Learning (CL) literature has long been driven by the goal of mitigating catastrophic forgetting.
EPIC: Rethinking Trust Regions for Autoregressive LLM Policies via Exact-KL Prefix Importance-Ratio Clipping

First: 2026-10-21T16:37:09+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T16:37:09+00:00
Comments: 27 pages. Code: https://github.com/LiChengxi666/EPIC
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2 · Code3
Abstract
Reinforcement learning (RL) based on PPO-style clipping has become an effective approach to improving the reasoning capabilities of large language models (LLMs). However, standard token-level clipping measures policy change only through the conditional probability of the current token, while the prefix conditioning context is itself generated by the policy. This mismatch becomes especially consequential in long-response and off-policy training. We reformulate autoregressive generation as a finite-horizon Markov decision process and derive its performance-difference identity under behavior-policy sampling. The resulting change of measure identifies the cumulative likelihood ratio of the generated prefix as the exact importance weight for each stepwise policy-improvement term. Starting from the monotonic-improvement principle of trust-region policy optimization, we then construct a prefix-level trust region using the exact reverse-KL coordinate $r-1-\log r$, whose behavior-policy expectation equals the corresponding prefix-distribution KL divergence. This yields EPIC, a PPO-style sequence-clipping method that directly regulates prefix-level policy drift without relying on a local quadratic approximation or expert-routing replay. Experiments on off-policy reinforcement learning with a sparse mixture-of-experts language model show that EPIC consistently outperforms standard and sequence-level clipping baselines across mathematical-reasoning benchmarks, while remaining competitive with routing-replay-based alternatives. Code is available at https://github.com/LiChengxi666/EPIC.

CORD: Conflict-Aware Optimization for Joint RL and Distillation in Agents
Authors: Wenhao Yu, Zhengcheng Wang, Jiahong Liu, Zexuan Qiu, Minda Hu, Shaohang Wei, Jiafei Lyu, Junyan Lyu, Haobo Fu, Irwin King
First: 2026-10-21T16:19:22+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T16:19:22+00:00

Abstract
Joint reinforcement learning and self-distillation combines task rewards with dense teacher guidance for agent training, but their gradients can partly cancel. A mixing coefficient controls relative scale without selectively removing opposing components. We present CORD, a general framework for conflict-aware optimization of two objectives. CORD separates the choice of protected objective from the condition that triggers projection, and combines the gradients once per optimizer mini-batch. A nearest-point formulation yields a closed-form update and characterizes its first-order effect on the protected objective before optimizer transformations. Gradient diagnostics connect conflict in agent training to lost local optimization progress and motivate selective correction. As a gradient-combination mechanism, CORD can be added to different joint training recipes while retaining their objectives. Across three such recipes, it improves ALFWorld success by 1.5-15.9 percentage points. On WebShop, it improves two of the three pairs, with SDAR rising from 60.7% to 64.6% success. Our analyses explain why protection direction and projection gate must be chosen together, and why the distillation objective and its mixing weight remain consequential after projection.

Abstract
Speculative decoding greatly increases the interactivity of autoregressive language models by trading off computation for extra tokens generated in a single forward pass. Factorized draft models are especially efficient because they predict future-token marginals in parallel, but their independence assumption causes acceptance rates to degrade sharply as the speculative budget grows. We analyze this limitation and introduce Weaver, a lightweight autoregressive adapter that constructs proposal trees from the top-K marginals of a factorized drafter. By combining these model and systems contributions we achieve a 4.08-fold speedup over autoregressive decoding, and outperform a highly optimized DFlash baseline by 16.3%.

Abstract
Can LLM agents make contributions to frontier computer science R&D? We introduce FrontierCS 2, a benchmark of 48 open-ended tasks. We take a paper and the code its authors released and delete the paper's contribution. The deleted code becomes the human reference that sets the bar. Earlier open-ended benchmarks give each agent a problem statement and grade one program after the run. FrontierCS 2 places the agent inside a research environment. The agent inherits a working repository and may submit a patch at any point. For each patch, a judge returns a development-suite score but keeps the evaluator, the held-out final suite and the reference private, and grades the final submission on that suite against the reference. The tasks span 13 domains, from machine-learning systems and high-performance computing to databases, security and robotics. Beyond these 48 tasks, FrontierCS 2 keeps a private set of more than 100 tasks, to be announced in the future. We evaluate 12 agents. The best one is Claude Opus 5.5, passing 58% of its runs. No run passes on 16 tasks. A higher score is not the same as a pass: most runs that beat the reference on the combined final score still fall short on some metric or workload. On a shared capability scale for agents and tasks, the median task is roughly as difficult as what the strongest agent can handle.

Abstract
Autonomous mobile robots operating in dense crowds and cluttered sites require $360^\circ$ perception to react to surrounding hazards. While panoramic LiDAR offers full-surround coverage, learning representations within reinforcement learning (RL) is sample-inefficient under sparse rewards, and existing 3D pretrained point-cloud encoders are too memory-heavy for massively parallel RL simulation. We present RangeNav, a compact (183k parameters) LiDAR range-image encoder pretrained entirely on synthetic data. RangeNav uses anisotropic convolutions and circular padding to handle the extreme aspect ratio and azimuthal seam of range images. In simulation, adding semantic and BEV supervision increases success rates over a reconstruction-only baseline by an average of 32.03 and 23.73 percentage points (pp) in procedurally generated training and out-of-distribution realistic test environments, respectively. Zero-shot real-world deployments on a Unitree B2-W quadruped demonstrate robust navigation through dense crowds, low-light settings, and construction zones where limited-field-of-view depth policies struggle. Code and models will be open-sourced.

Synthesis and Verification of Register Automata over Data Sequences (Technical Report)
Authors: Chih-Duo Hong, Hongjian Jiang, Anthony W. Lin, Oliver Markgraf, Julian Parsert, Tony Tan
First: 2025-11-24T13:36:45+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T13:42:27+00:00
Abs · PDF · Code1 · Code2
Abstract
Data sequences, such as time series, traces, and sensor streams, are words over large or infinite data domains. Their properties often depend on comparisons between values at different positions, which finite automata cannot express directly. We study synthesis and verification for Deterministic Register Automata (DRAs) over ordered data sequences. DRAs combine finite control with registers for remembered data values, providing compact symbolic descriptions of quantitative sequence languages. Our technical core is a local-search synthesis procedure and an exact robustness checker for DRAs. We fix the alphabet theory to ordered rationals $(\Q;<,=)$ with rational guard constants and use non-negative affine accumulator costs. For a fixed number of DRA registers, the checker runs in polynomial time; its dependence on that number is exponential. It reduces robustness to coverability and then to a shortest-path problem. Since the search ranges over $\Q^+$, it produces variable-length witnesses and, for RAA-definable alignment costs such as DTW, explores a counterexample space with no a priori length bound. As an application, we use learned DRAs as interpretable surrogates for queried neural sequence classifiers. A probably approximately correct (PAC) validation loop refines rejected candidates, returns genuine non-robustness witnesses for the classifier when found, and accepts a surrogate only with statistical guarantees for fidelity and local stability. We implement the framework in $\textsc{RAExc}$, enabling the synthesis of interpretable automata and the verification of their robustness. We evaluate the approach on benchmark languages and sequentialized MNIST, demonstrating high accuracy while verifying the robustness of the learned automata.

Abstract
Vision-language-action (VLA) policies need to recognize gaps in their training knowledge to request useful demonstrations or assistance in new situations, especially when failures are costly. Disagreement within an ensemble of independently trained policies can guide these decisions, but requires training and running multiple policies. We propose post-hoc kernel-variance heads that estimate epistemic uncertainty from the embeddings of a single policy. Each head computes the conditional variance of a Gaussian process conditioned on embeddings from the training demonstrations. Fitting requires no regression targets or gradient updates and leaves the policy unchanged. In experiments with SmolVLA on LIBERO-10, the kernel variance heads perform competitively with two-policy ensemble scores in task-level correlation with task success, in demonstration acquisition during active learning, and in referral to a stronger supervisor policy as a proxy for human assistance. For SmolVLA, the head on the vision-language backbone reduces uncertainty-scoring time by up to approximately a factor of 50 compared with ensemble scores, while also substantially reducing setup costs. We also show that the established evaluation protocol on LIBERO-10 primarily rewards recognition of coverage differences between tasks, while execution variability weakens assessment of within-task rankings. Thus, controlled perturbations provide a complementary evaluation: modality-specific heads identify unfamiliar camera, instruction, or state inputs, while fused heads can detect unfamiliar combinations of individually familiar inputs. With a supervisor proxy trained on the evaluated perturbation families, the decoder head improves within-task referral over ensemble disagreement. These findings show that useful uncertainty estimates for adaptive robot learning can be obtained from a single policy.

Quantum f-divergences from retrodiction: a unifying classical variational principle
Authors: Francesco Buscemi
First: 2026-10-21T13:09:11+00:00 · Latest: 2026-10-21T13:09:11+00:00
Comments: 18 pages, 2 figures; preliminary version, comments welcome; 100th arXiv post

Abstract
The same classical f-divergence admits inequivalent extensions to quantum states. The question is whether these extensions can be obtained from a common operational variational principle. Here we generalize Matsumoto's reverse-test optimization by introducing a reference prior state. More explicitly, we minimize the statistical distinguishability between a forward and a reverse prepare-and-measure process, related by retrodiction through the chosen reference and subject to prescribed quantum averages. Distinguishability is quantified by the classical f-divergence between their complete records. For faithful states in finite dimension, we prove that the same spectral implementation attains the minimum simultaneously for every operator-convex f. Varying the reference allows us to interpolate between standard and maximal quantum f-divergences of the prescribed states, passing through the geodesic family. More precisely, a flat reference yields the standard quantum f-divergences, thereby giving the Nussbaum-Szkoła distributions a variational characterization as optimal records. Choosing either prescribed state as reference recovers maximal quantum f-divergences and Matsumoto's reverse tests, while power references realize the geodesic interpolation. In the relative-entropy case, this gives the passage from Umegaki to Belavkin-Staszewski relative entropy by changing the prior for retrodiction.

Abstract
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have demonstrated remarkable effectiveness on graph-based tasks. However, their predictive confidence is often miscalibrated, typically exhibiting under-confidence, which harms the reliability of their decisions. Existing calibration methods for GNNs normally introduce additional calibration components, which fail to capture the intrinsic relationship between the model and the prediction confidence, resulting in limited theoretical guarantees and increased computational overhead. Abstract
